9. Passage Commentary — Herman Melville: “Bartleby, the Scrivener”

This passage comes from Bartleby, the Scrivener by Herman Melville. The story is narrated by a Wall Street lawyer who employs Bartleby as a copyist. At first quiet and efficient, Bartleby gradually begins to refuse tasks with the phrase "I would prefer not to." This passage is significant because it introduces Bartleby's defining act of passive resistance and destabilizes the narrator's sense of authority, reason, and moral order.

One of the most striking features of the passage is Bartleby's language. His refusal is neither aggressive nor emotional; instead, it is expressed with calm politeness. The phrase "I would prefer not to" avoids direct confrontation while remaining absolute. By framing refusal as a matter of preference rather than defiance, Bartleby removes the possibility of argument. The language is grammatically courteous but logically paralyzing, revealing the power of passivity.

Characterization in the passage depends largely on contrast. Bartleby appears emotionally blank and inscrutable, offering no explanation for his behavior. In contrast, the narrator responds with confusion, rationalization, and increasing anxiety. Accustomed to obedience and routine, the lawyer attempts to reason with Bartleby, assuming that all behavior must have a logical motive. Bartleby's refusal undermines this assumption, exposing the limits of the narrator's rational worldview.

The conflict introduced in this passage is not physical or dramatic, but philosophical. Bartleby does not rebel openly against authority; instead, he simply withdraws cooperation. This form of resistance is deeply unsettling because it cannot be punished or corrected easily. The narrator's authority depends on shared social expectations about work, duty, and obedience. When Bartleby quietly steps outside those expectations, the entire system begins to fail.

The narrative point of view intensifies the passage's effect. Told in the first person, the story filters Bartleby entirely through the lawyer's perspective. The reader never gains direct access to Bartleby's inner thoughts, reinforcing his mystery. At the same time, the narrator's attempt to explain Bartleby through charity, psychology, or moral reasoning reveals more about the narrator's need for control than about Bartleby himself. This limited perspective encourages readers to question the narrator's interpretation of events.

Setting also contributes to the passage's meaning. The Wall Street office represents order, productivity, and bureaucratic routine. Bartleby's refusal disrupts this environment without visible force. His stillness contrasts sharply with the mechanical copying work expected of him, suggesting a rejection of dehumanizing labor. The passage hints at a broader critique of modern work culture, in which individuals are reduced to functions.

Thesis statement: This passage is central to Bartleby, the Scrivener because Bartleby's quiet refusal introduces a form of passive resistance that exposes the fragility of authority, rational order, and moral certainty within modern bureaucratic life.

In the context of the story as a whole, this moment sets in motion the narrator's growing obsession with Bartleby and his eventual moral crisis. Bartleby's refusal does not seek change or justice; it simply withdraws participation. Melville suggests that such withdrawal is both unsettling and tragic, revealing a world in which human connection, purpose, and meaning have eroded. The passage therefore captures the story's enduring power: the ability of silence and noncompliance to challenge the foundations of social order.

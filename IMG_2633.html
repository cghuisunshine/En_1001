Desperation and Decision in Barn Burning and Sonny’s Blues

In literature, characters often avoid making difficult decisions as long as they believe other options still exist. Comfort, habit, and fear allow delay. However, when pressure increases and alternatives disappear, desperation forces action. In such moments, decisions—whether good or bad—become unavoidable. This idea is clearly illustrated in Barn Burning and Sonny's Blues. Although the stories differ in setting, tone, and narrative style, both demonstrate that desperation is the surest path to decision-making. When characters feel trapped by moral conflict or emotional pain, they are compelled to act, and these actions reveal their values, limitations, and growth.

In Barn Burning, young Sarty Snopes lives under constant pressure created by his father, Abner Snopes. Abner is a violent and proud man who responds to any perceived insult by burning property. His actions repeatedly place the family in danger and force them to move from place to place. From the beginning of the story, Sarty experiences intense inner conflict. On one hand, he feels loyalty to his father and understands that family unity is expected of him. On the other hand, he senses that his father's actions are wrong and unjust. At first, Sarty attempts to avoid choosing between these two forces by remaining silent and obedient.

As the story progresses, Sarty's desperation increases. Abner's behavior shows no sign of change, and another barn burning becomes inevitable. At this point, Sarty's options become extremely limited. He can either remain loyal to his father and allow another act of injustice to occur, or he can betray his family in order to prevent harm. Faced with this pressure, Sarty makes a decisive choice to warn the landowner about the planned fire. This decision is not made calmly or confidently; it is made in fear, confusion, and emotional pain. The choice costs Sarty his family and his sense of belonging, but it represents moral growth. Faulkner shows that desperation forces Sarty to act, and in doing so, reveals Abner's tragic flaw—his stubborn pride and cruelty—as well as Sarty's painful step toward independence.

A different but equally powerful form of desperation appears in Sonny's Blues. In this story, the pressure is emotional rather than physical. The narrator, Sonny's older brother, believes strongly in discipline, responsibility, and emotional restraint. He thinks that following rules and avoiding strong emotions can protect people from suffering. Because of this belief, he keeps emotional distance from Sonny, whose life is shaped by music, addiction, and pain. For much of the story, the narrator avoids making any deep emotional decision about Sonny. As long as he maintains distance, he believes he can avoid pain.

The narrator's desperation becomes clear during Sonny's jazz performance near the end of the story. In this moment, he is confronted with Sonny's suffering in a way that cannot be ignored or explained away. Music expresses pain that words cannot. The narrator's options suddenly narrow: he can either continue judging Sonny and remain emotionally distant, or he can accept Sonny's suffering and recognize music as a necessary way of surviving that suffering. Under this emotional pressure, the narrator makes a decisive choice to listen and understand. This decision is quiet and internal, but it is deeply significant. Baldwin shows that desperation does not always lead to dramatic action; sometimes it leads to emotional openness and empathy. The narrator's decision changes how he understands his brother and allows a deeper human connection to form.

When Barn Burning and Sonny's Blues are considered together, the role of desperation becomes clear. In Barn Burning, desperation leads to a public and irreversible action that separates Sarty from his family. In Sonny's Blues, desperation leads to an internal shift in perspective that allows understanding and reconciliation. In both cases, characters act decisively only when avoidance is no longer possible. As long as characters believe they can escape conflict, they delay action. When desperation removes this possibility, decision becomes unavoidable. These stories also show that desperation does not guarantee a good outcome. Sarty's decision brings loss and uncertainty, while the narrator's decision does not erase Sonny's struggles. What desperation guarantees is not success, but clarity and action.

In conclusion, Barn Burning and Sonny's Blues strongly support the idea that desperation is the surest path to making a decision, good or bad. When characters feel trapped by moral conflict or emotional pain, they are forced to act. These decisions expose character more clearly than moments of comfort ever could. Through their portrayal of desperation, Faulkner and Baldwin show that meaningful choices in literature often arise not from freedom, but from necessity.

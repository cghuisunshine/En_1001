The Effect of Narrative Point of View in Hills Like White Elephants and Barn Burning

In short fiction, the narrator's choice of point of view strongly shapes how readers understand characters, conflict, and meaning. Some narrators provide little guidance, while others offer detailed insight into thoughts and emotions. This contrast is clearly visible in Ernest Hemingway's Hills Like White Elephants and William Faulkner's Barn Burning. Hemingway's narrator is restrained and withholding, revealing very little beyond dialogue and setting, while Faulkner's narrator is more expressive and psychologically detailed. Although both stories explore moral and emotional conflict, the different narrative approaches create very different reading experiences. Together, these works demonstrate that point of view determines how readers interpret character, assign moral judgment, and engage with the story.

In Hills Like White Elephants, Hemingway uses a limited and objective narrative style that gives readers almost no access to the characters' inner thoughts. The narrator describes only what can be seen or heard, such as the landscape, gestures, and dialogue between the man and the woman, Jig. Crucially, the central issue of the story—the abortion—is never directly named. By withholding this information, Hemingway forces readers to infer meaning from what is said and, more importantly, from what is avoided. The narrator's refusal to explain motives or emotions creates ambiguity and tension from the opening of the story.

This narrative restraint has a powerful effect on the reader. Because the narrator does not judge the characters, readers must evaluate the relationship on their own. The man repeatedly claims that the operation is "simple" and insists that he only wants what Jig wants. Without narrative commentary, these statements appear calm on the surface, yet their repetition suggests pressure and manipulation. Jig's increasing discomfort and her final plea for the man to stop talking reveal emotional imbalance without explicit explanation. Hemingway's point of view therefore places responsibility on the reader to interpret tone, silence, and subtext. What is left unsaid becomes as meaningful as what is spoken, highlighting the couple's inability to communicate honestly.

In contrast, Faulkner's Barn Burning employs a much more detailed and emotionally involved narrative perspective. The story is told in the third person but closely follows the thoughts and feelings of young Sarty Snopes. From the opening courtroom scene, readers are given direct access to Sarty's fear, confusion, and inner conflict. The narrator frequently explains Sarty's thoughts, memories, and moral struggles, guiding the reader through his emotional development. This "chatty" narration reduces ambiguity and directs the reader's moral response.

Faulkner's narrative choice creates strong emotional engagement with Sarty. By revealing his internal struggle between loyalty to his father and his sense of justice, the narrator encourages readers to sympathize with the boy's difficult position. Unlike Hemingway's detached narrator, Faulkner's narrator clearly frames Abner Snopes as destructive and morally wrong through both action and description. However, even in this more revealing style, some elements remain unsaid. Abner's inner thoughts are never explored, keeping him distant and unsympathetic. This selective focus ensures that moral attention remains on Sarty's decision rather than on his father's motivations.

When the two stories are compared, the effects of their different narrative choices become clear. Hemingway's minimalism creates uncertainty and requires active interpretation, while Faulkner's detailed narration offers clarity and emotional guidance. In Hills Like White Elephants, readers must decide how to judge the characters without assistance, which mirrors the characters' emotional isolation. In Barn Burning, readers are guided toward understanding Sarty's moral growth, making his final decision feel both inevitable and meaningful. Each approach shapes how readers engage with the story's central conflict.

Despite their differences, both authors use point of view strategically to reinforce theme. Hemingway's silence emphasizes the difficulty of communication and the imbalance of power within the relationship. Faulkner's psychological detail emphasizes moral responsibility and the pain of choosing between right and wrong. In both cases, narration is not neutral. The narrator's presence or absence controls what the reader knows, feels, and judges.

In conclusion, Hills Like White Elephants and Barn Burning demonstrate how narrative point of view shapes the reader's experience of a story. Hemingway's restrained narration relies on omission and ambiguity, forcing readers to construct meaning from silence. Faulkner's more expressive narration provides emotional depth and moral clarity through close psychological focus. Together, these stories show that what a narrator chooses to reveal is just as important as what is left unsaid, and that point of view is a powerful tool in shaping interpretation and understanding.

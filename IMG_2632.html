Tragic Flaws Beyond Tragedy in Barn Burning and Sonny’s Blues

Tragic flaws are often associated with classical tragedy, where a character's weakness leads to downfall or death. However, literature shows that tragic flaws are not limited to tragic genres. Characters in non-tragic works can also reveal deep personal limitations through their choices and conflicts. William Faulkner's Barn Burning and James Baldwin's Sonny's Blues both demonstrate this idea. Although neither story is a traditional tragedy, each reveals tragic flaws through characters who struggle with pride, fear, and emotional limitation. These flaws shape their actions and relationships, showing that tragedy lies not only in outcomes, but in human weakness itself.

In Barn Burning, Abner Snopes clearly exhibits a tragic flaw rooted in pride and stubbornness. Abner is unable to accept any form of authority and responds to perceived injustice with violence. His repeated acts of burning barns are not merely criminal behavior, but expressions of his refusal to submit to social order. This flaw dominates his character and dictates his actions. Unlike a tragic hero in classical drama, Abner is not presented as noble or admirable. However, his inability to change reveals a deeply tragic limitation: he is trapped by his own pride and resentment.

Abner's tragic flaw affects not only himself, but also his son, Sarty. Sarty's internal conflict reveals another tragic flaw—his painful divided loyalty. He struggles between obedience to his father and his sense of justice. While Sarty ultimately makes a morally responsible choice by warning the landowner, his decision comes at great emotional cost. He loses his family and his sense of belonging. Faulkner shows that even when a character chooses what is morally right, tragic flaws can still shape suffering. The story is not a tragedy in form, yet it reveals how deeply human weakness can damage lives.

A different type of tragic flaw appears in Sonny's Blues. The narrator, Sonny's older brother, is not violent or cruel, but he suffers from emotional rigidity and fear of suffering. His tragic flaw lies in his belief that order, discipline, and emotional distance can protect him from pain. Because of this belief, he judges Sonny's lifestyle and distances himself from his brother's struggles with addiction and music. This flaw does not lead to death or destruction, but it creates emotional separation and misunderstanding.

Throughout the story, the narrator resists understanding Sonny's need for music as a form of survival. He believes that suffering can be controlled or avoided through responsibility and restraint. This flawed belief prevents him from offering genuine support to Sonny. Baldwin presents this limitation as tragic because it reflects a failure of empathy. The narrator's flaw isolates him emotionally and delays connection with his brother, even though reconciliation remains possible.

The narrator's tragic flaw is finally revealed and confronted during Sonny's jazz performance. As he listens to the music, he realizes that suffering cannot be avoided, but must be expressed and shared. This moment of recognition does not erase Sonny's addiction or guarantee happiness. However, it exposes the narrator's earlier blindness and emotional limitation. Baldwin shows that tragic flaws can exist even in stories that allow growth and understanding. The flaw is tragic not because it leads to death, but because it limits human connection.

When Barn Burning and Sonny's Blues are considered together, they demonstrate that tragic flaws operate beyond the boundaries of tragic genre. Abner's pride leads to destruction and isolation, while Sarty's divided loyalty causes pain even as it leads to moral growth. Similarly, the narrator in Sonny's Blues suffers from emotional blindness that delays understanding and compassion. None of these characters fit neatly into the structure of classical tragedy, yet their flaws deeply shape their lives and relationships.

The key difference between tragic genre and tragic flaw lies in outcome rather than character. Tragedy traditionally ends in irreversible loss, while these stories allow moments of change or survival. However, the presence of tragic flaws remains central. Faulkner and Baldwin both suggest that human weakness does not disappear simply because a story avoids a tragic ending. Instead, tragic flaws continue to influence behavior, conflict, and meaning.

In conclusion, Barn Burning and Sonny's Blues support the idea that tragedy is not the only genre in which characters reveal a tragic flaw. Through pride, fear, and emotional limitation, characters expose weaknesses that shape their actions and relationships. These flaws may not lead to death or complete ruin, but they still cause suffering and loss. By presenting tragic flaws in non-tragic narratives, Faulkner and Baldwin show that tragedy exists not only in genre, but in the enduring limitations of human nature.

12. Passage Commentary — William Faulkner: “Barn Burning”

This passage comes from Barn Burning by William Faulkner. The story follows young Colonel Sartoris Snopes, known as Sarty, as he struggles to reconcile loyalty to his father with an emerging sense of justice. His father, Abner Snopes, repeatedly commits acts of arson as a form of revenge against those he believes have wronged him. This passage is significant because it marks the climax of Sarty's moral conflict and his first independent ethical choice.

One of the most striking features of the passage is Sarty's intense internal struggle. Throughout the story, he has been taught that family loyalty is absolute, reinforced by his father's demand that he "stick to your blood." In this passage, however, Sarty becomes increasingly aware that such loyalty requires complicity in injustice. His fear and hesitation reflect not weakness, but the difficulty of breaking away from a deeply ingrained moral code imposed by authority and violence.

Characterization is sharply drawn through contrast between father and son. Abner Snopes is depicted as rigid, bitter, and driven by resentment toward social authority. His actions are not impulsive but deliberate, suggesting that arson is a calculated assertion of power rather than an emotional outburst. Sarty, by contrast, is sensitive and morally alert. In this passage, his decision to act against his father represents a rejection of Abner's worldview and an affirmation of a broader ethical responsibility.

The conflict in the passage extends beyond the family to reflect larger social tensions. Abner's hostility toward landowners such as Major de Spain reveals class resentment and a desire to assert equality through destruction. However, Faulkner complicates this resentment by showing that Abner's resistance is rooted less in justice than in pride and cruelty. Sarty's decision to warn Major de Spain suggests a recognition that true justice cannot be achieved through revenge or violence.

Faulkner's narrative point of view intensifies the passage's emotional impact. Although the story is told in the third person, it is closely aligned with Sarty's consciousness. The reader experiences events through his fear, confusion, and growing clarity. Faulkner's long, complex sentences mirror the pressure Sarty feels, while moments of abrupt action reflect the urgency of his choice.

Setting also plays an important symbolic role. The dark rural landscape and the looming barn represent secrecy, destruction, and inherited violence. When Sarty breaks away and runs toward the house to warn its owner, movement itself becomes symbolic. His physical flight parallels his moral escape from his father's influence.

Thesis statement: This passage is central to Barn Burning because it depicts Sarty's decisive break from inherited loyalty toward an independent moral judgment, marking his transition from obedience to ethical responsibility.

In the context of the story as a whole, this moment explains Sarty's final isolation. By choosing justice over blood, he loses his family but gains moral autonomy. Faulkner presents this choice as painful and irreversible, suggesting that moral growth often requires separation and loss. The passage therefore captures the story's central insight: that true integrity may demand the rejection of inherited authority, even at great personal cost.

4. Passage Commentary — Amy Tan: “Rules of the Game”

This passage comes from Rules of the Game, a story that explores the relationship between a Chinese American mother and her daughter, Waverly Jong. Narrated from Waverly's perspective, the story traces her rise as a chess prodigy and her growing desire for independence from her mother's control. This passage is significant because it marks the turning point where chess, once a source of empowerment, becomes the site of open conflict, revealing the tension between individual identity and parental authority.

One of the most striking aspects of the passage is the way chess functions as a symbol of power. Earlier in the story, Waverly learns the concept of "invisible strength," which allows her to win games through strategy and restraint. In this passage, however, that invisible strength collapses when she confronts her mother directly. By openly challenging her mother in public, Waverly breaks the very rules that once gave her control. This moment shows that strategy without obedience no longer works within the family structure.

Characterization is central to the passage's impact. Waverly's mother is portrayed as authoritative, proud, and deeply invested in her daughter's success. Her constant public display of Waverly's achievements reflects both cultural pride and personal ambition. Waverly, on the other hand, begins the story eager to please but grows increasingly resentful. In this passage, her frustration surfaces openly, revealing her desire to be recognized as an individual rather than as an extension of her mother.

The conflict between mother and daughter is intensified by the public setting. The argument occurs in a space where the mother's authority has previously gone unquestioned. By challenging her mother in front of others, Waverly violates not only family rules but cultural expectations of obedience and respect. This public confrontation transforms a private power struggle into a decisive break, making reconciliation impossible in the moment.

The first-person narrative point of view deepens the emotional impact of the passage. Because the story is told from Waverly's perspective, the reader experiences her anger and humiliation directly. However, the narration also reveals her immaturity. While Waverly believes she is asserting independence, her reaction is impulsive rather than strategic. This irony reinforces the story's central idea that understanding power does not necessarily mean understanding responsibility.

Chess imagery continues to shape the meaning of the passage. When Waverly runs away mentally and emotionally, she imagines a final chessboard where she faces her mother as an opponent. This imagined game suggests that Waverly now views their relationship in purely competitive terms. The cooperative aspects of learning, guidance, and sacrifice have disappeared, replaced by opposition and separation.

Thesis statement: This passage is central to Rules of the Game because it reveals how Waverly's pursuit of independence turns the rules that once empowered her into instruments of conflict, highlighting the difficulty of balancing personal identity with familial and cultural expectations.

In the context of the story as a whole, this moment explains why Waverly ultimately loses both her chess confidence and her sense of direction. By rejecting her mother without fully understanding the cost, she discovers that independence can also mean isolation. Amy Tan uses this passage to show that growing up involves not only learning how to win, but learning when resistance becomes self-defeating.

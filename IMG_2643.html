11. Passage Commentary — Ernest Hemingway: “Hills Like White Elephants”

This passage comes from Hills Like White Elephants by Ernest Hemingway. Set at a railway station in Spain, the story presents a conversation between an American man and a young woman, Jig, as they wait for a train. Although the subject of abortion is never stated directly, it dominates their exchange. This passage is significant because it exposes the emotional imbalance and communication breakdown at the heart of the relationship, revealing how avoidance and indirect language replace genuine understanding.

One of the most striking features of the passage is Hemingway's use of dialogue to convey conflict. The characters speak in short, repetitive exchanges that circle around the same issue without ever naming it. The American insists that the decision is simple and claims he only wants what Jig wants, yet his repeated reassurances function as pressure rather than comfort. Jig's plea for him to stop talking signals emotional exhaustion and highlights the futility of their conversation.

Characterization in the passage emerges entirely through speech. The American appears rational, controlled, and emotionally detached. He frames the situation as a minor inconvenience, emphasizing ease and normality. Jig, by contrast, becomes increasingly aware of the emotional cost of the decision. Her responses grow sharper and more ironic, suggesting that she understands the implications more deeply than he does. The imbalance in their communication reflects an imbalance in power: the American dominates the conversation, while Jig struggles to assert her feelings.

The setting intensifies the passage's meaning. The couple sits between two landscapes—one fertile and life-giving, the other dry and barren. This physical division mirrors Jig's emotional dilemma and underscores the story's central choice. The railway station itself is a place of transition, emphasizing that a decision must be made, even if it is never openly acknowledged. The stillness of the setting contrasts with the tension beneath the dialogue.

Hemingway's narrative style is essential to the passage's effect. The third-person objective point of view offers no access to the characters' inner thoughts, forcing readers to infer meaning from tone, repetition, and omission. This technique exemplifies Hemingway's "iceberg theory," in which the most important elements of the story remain submerged. The silence surrounding the abortion makes its presence more powerful, as the reader becomes acutely aware of what is not being said.

The passage also contributes to the story's unresolved ending. Jig's final request for silence does not resolve the conflict but temporarily halts it. The American's response suggests that he believes the conversation—and perhaps the problem—can simply be managed away. This reinforces the story's bleak view of communication: talking does not necessarily lead to understanding, and silence may only deepen emotional distance.

Thesis statement: This passage is central to Hills Like White Elephants because it reveals how indirect language and emotional avoidance prevent genuine communication, exposing the power imbalance between the characters and underscoring the story's theme of unresolved choice.

In the context of the story as a whole, the passage clarifies that the conflict is not only about a medical decision but about the future of the relationship. Hemingway suggests that the inability to speak honestly reflects a deeper incompatibility. The story ends without resolution, reinforcing the idea that some choices, once avoided or imposed, permanently alter human connection. The passage therefore captures the quiet tragedy of a relationship strained by silence and unequal understanding.

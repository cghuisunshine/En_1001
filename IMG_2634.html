Beginnings That Contain the Whole: Structure in Hills Like White Elephants and Barn Burning

If a story is truly a self-contained whole, its opening must do more than simply introduce characters or setting. A strong beginning prepares the reader for the themes, conflicts, and outcomes that will follow, even if these elements are not immediately clear. Ernest Hemingway's Hills Like White Elephants and William Faulkner's Barn Burning both demonstrate this structural principle. Although the two stories differ greatly in style and narration, each uses its opening to suggest the central conflicts and moral questions that shape the entire work. Through setting, tone, and early tension, both openings quietly contain the core of the story that unfolds.

The opening of Hills Like White Elephants is brief and restrained, yet it establishes the story's central concerns almost immediately. The first description focuses on the setting: a dry, barren landscape divided by a railway station, with hills in the distance that resemble white elephants. This physical division mirrors the emotional and moral division between the two characters. Even before the topic of their conversation becomes clear, the setting suggests separation, uncertainty, and choice. The contrast between the dry side of the valley and the fertile land beyond hints at the possibility of different futures, a theme that runs throughout the story.

Hemingway's opening also establishes the narrative style that defines the entire work. The narrator offers no insight into the characters' thoughts or feelings and relies almost entirely on dialogue. This lack of explanation signals to the reader that meaning will not be given directly, but must be inferred. From the start, the conversation feels strained and indirect, suggesting unresolved tension. Although the word "abortion" is never mentioned, the opening prepares the reader to expect a conflict that will remain largely unspoken. In this way, the beginning reflects the story's overall structure: silence, omission, and ambiguity are not accidental, but central to its meaning.

As the story progresses, the elements introduced in the opening continue to shape interpretation. The division in the landscape parallels the emotional distance between the couple, and the indirect dialogue reflects their inability to communicate honestly. The opening's suggestion of choice becomes the core conflict of the story. By the end, nothing is resolved in a clear or comforting way, reinforcing the uncertainty present from the first paragraph. Hemingway's opening thus functions as a condensed version of the entire story, quietly establishing the themes of choice, imbalance, and emotional isolation that define the work as a whole.

Similarly, the opening of Barn Burning suggests the major conflicts and themes that dominate the story. Faulkner begins with a courtroom scene in which young Sarty Snopes is called to testify against his father, Abner. This immediate introduction of legal authority, family loyalty, and moral conflict prepares the reader for the struggle that will define the story. From the first page, Sarty is caught between obedience to his father and his sense of justice. This tension becomes the central issue of the narrative.

The opening also establishes the story's moral tone and narrative focus. Faulkner uses a close third-person perspective that follows Sarty's thoughts and emotions, allowing readers to experience his fear and confusion. The emphasis on Sarty's inner conflict signals that the story will focus less on external events and more on moral choice. At the same time, Abner's character is introduced through his actions and reputation rather than explanation, suggesting his role as a force of destruction rather than a sympathetic figure. These early choices shape how readers judge later events.

As Barn Burning develops, the elements introduced in the opening continue to guide the story. The courtroom scene foreshadows repeated confrontations with authority, while Sarty's early conflict prepares readers for his final decision to betray his father. The opening's focus on justice versus loyalty becomes the driving force behind every major event. By the end of the story, Sarty's choice feels inevitable because it has been prepared structurally from the beginning. Faulkner's opening thus reinforces the idea that the story is a self-contained whole, with its core conflict present from the first scene.

When Hills Like White Elephants and Barn Burning are considered together, their openings reveal different approaches to the same structural goal. Hemingway uses understatement and setting to suggest meaning, while Faulkner uses direct conflict and psychological depth. Despite these differences, both openings perform the same essential function: they prepare readers for the themes, conflicts, and outcomes that follow. Neither opening explains everything, but both contain the seeds of the entire story.

In conclusion, if a story is a self-contained whole, its opening must suggest the elements that will define it. Both Hills Like White Elephants and Barn Burning demonstrate this principle through carefully constructed beginnings. By establishing conflict, tone, and theme early on, Hemingway and Faulkner ensure that their stories develop organically from their openings. These works show that an effective beginning does not give away the story's meaning, but quietly holds it, allowing the reader to discover how the whole unfolds from the start.

Nature as an Active Force in Hills Like White Elephants and Barn Burning

In many works of literature, nature appears at first to function merely as background or setting. However, in some stories, the natural environment plays a more active role by shaping conflict, influencing character decisions, and reinforcing central themes. Ernest Hemingway's Hills Like White Elephants and William Faulkner's Barn Burning both use nature in this way. Although nature in these stories does not speak or act like a human character, it functions as a powerful presence that reflects emotional states and directs the course of events. To a significant extent, nature operates as a character by interacting with human conflict and deepening the meaning of each story.

In Hills Like White Elephants, nature is central to the story's meaning from the opening description. The story begins at a train station surrounded by a dry, barren landscape, with distant hills that resemble white elephants. This setting immediately suggests division and contrast. On one side of the station, the land is dry and lifeless; on the other side, there is fertile land with trees and a river. This physical separation mirrors the emotional and moral divide between the man and Jig as they discuss a life-changing decision. Nature silently presents two possible futures, making it an active force in the story rather than a neutral backdrop.

The hills themselves take on symbolic importance and influence the characters' interaction. Jig's observation that the hills look like white elephants introduces the idea of something valuable yet unwanted, reflecting how the pregnancy is viewed differently by the two characters. The man dismisses Jig's comment, revealing his emotional detachment. Nature thus exposes the imbalance in their relationship without direct explanation. The heat, dryness, and stillness of the setting contribute to the tension and discomfort of the conversation, reinforcing the emotional strain between the characters. Through these elements, nature participates in the conflict by shaping mood and highlighting unspoken tension.

As the story develops, the natural setting continues to guide interpretation. The contrast between barren and fertile land suggests the possibility of choice, while also emphasizing the difficulty of that choice. By the end of the story, the setting remains unchanged, reinforcing the uncertainty and lack of resolution. In this way, nature functions like a silent character: it frames the conflict, reflects emotional states, and reinforces the theme of difficult choice. While nature does not act independently, it plays a crucial role in shaping how readers understand the characters and their situation.

In Barn Burning, nature plays a more direct and destructive role in the narrative. The rural landscape, dusty roads, and repeated imagery of fire create a harsh environment that reflects Abner Snopes's violent nature. Fire, in particular, becomes a recurring natural force that drives the plot. Although fire is controlled by Abner, it behaves like an uncontrollable character that spreads destruction and fear. The presence of fire repeatedly forces the family to move and creates the conditions under which Sarty must confront moral choices.

The natural environment also shapes Sarty's emotional development. The open fields, dark night, and looming barns heighten the tension as Sarty struggles between loyalty to his father and his sense of justice. Nature amplifies the danger of Abner's actions and makes the consequences of silence impossible to ignore. The physical landscape becomes the space where moral conflict is acted out. Unlike a neutral setting, nature here interacts with human behavior and pushes the story toward its climax.

At the end of Barn Burning, nature plays a crucial role in Sarty's final decision. As Sarty runs into the night to warn the landowner, the darkness and open space emphasize his isolation and fear. The shots fired in the distance blend into the natural environment, leaving Sarty alone with the landscape. This moment suggests both loss and freedom. Nature absorbs the consequences of Sarty's choice and marks his separation from his family. In this sense, nature functions as a silent witness and participant in his moral awakening.

When these two stories are compared, it becomes clear that nature functions as a character to different extents. In Hills Like White Elephants, nature is symbolic and reflective, shaping mood and highlighting emotional conflict. In Barn Burning, nature is more active and threatening, driving events and intensifying moral struggle. In both cases, nature does more than provide a setting. It interacts with character decisions and reinforces central themes.

In conclusion, nature in Hills Like White Elephants and Barn Burning functions as a character to a significant extent. Although it does not possess consciousness or voice, nature shapes conflict, influences emotion, and directs the development of the story. Through symbolic landscapes and destructive forces, Hemingway and Faulkner demonstrate that nature can actively participate in narrative meaning. These stories show that nature, when used deliberately, becomes an essential presence rather than a passive background.
